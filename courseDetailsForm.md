## Course Details 


The course details is broken down into two sections in the CDF--under the header course details, and then under the header proposed changes. You will need to review both before commenting. Where information exists under proposed changes, disregard that element from under the course details header as it has been updated and replaced.

The critical components for this section are for: the clarity of the scope of the course, the language as it is communicating the aim to the students, and the alignment between the aspects of the course details (i.e., the course learning outcomes, aim, program outcomes).


### Aim

A course aim connects the course to the wider program aims, shows how it fits with other courses, and highlights its contribution to developing overall graduate qualities within the program.

Writing a course aim involves a clear understanding of several key elements, highlighted as follows:

- **Intent (WHY):** The purpose behind the course.
- **Content (WHAT will be taught):** The main topics and material covered.
- **Expected Learning (WHAT learners will learn):** The knowledge or skills students are expected to gain.
- **Program Fit (RELATIONSHIP):** How the course aligns with the overall program goals and graduate qualities.

A well-crafted course aim should concisely incorporate these elements, ideally in a brief statement not exceeding 100 words.

**Example**s

The following is a hypothetical example of a course aim of second year Consumer Behaviour Course in a Bachelor of Marketing program: 

_This course aims to equip learners with the knowledge and skills to analyse, and effectively influence consumer behaviour. Building upon concepts of marketing introduced earlier, the course delves into the psychological, social, and cultural processes that shape consumer decision-making, providing a foundation in buyer behaviour theories and models. By examining the consumer decision making process, learners will develop critical thinking and problem-solving skills essential for creating impactful marketing strategies. This course aligns with the program's intent to provide a comprehensive understanding of marketing and consumer behaviour, preparing learners to excel in a global market._


As it is probably clear from the example, there are different elements as follows:  

- Intent of course 
- What is the content? How it fits with subsequent or preceding content/courses. 
- What learners will learn, and the Graduate Qualities they will develop (if, for illustrative purposes, we assume problem solving and critical thinking are graduate qualities). 
- Program fit
This is just one example, and as you may have noticed the different elements of an aim can be in any order as long as it touches on all of them, and communicates clearly to the reader.

### Course Learning Outcomes

_CLOs play a crucial role in determining course content and shaping the variety and type of teaching and learning activities that learners will engage with._ 

As general principles, CLOs:

- **Must be assessable** to serve their primary function. Without the ability to assess them, CLOs cannot achieve their fundamental goal. 
- **Define expected outcomes**, detailing the knowledge, skills, and competencies learners must demonstrate via specific course assessments. 
- **Influence course structure**, playing a key role in deciding the course content and the variety of teaching and learning activities. 
- **Serve multiple key purposes**, including: 

- Outlining course content, 
- Guiding the selection of teaching and learning methods, 
- Forming the basis for assessment strategies, 
- Setting clear expectations for learners, 
- Aiding in achieving one or more Program Learning Outcomes (PLOs). 

- **Contribute to program goals**, aiming to fulfill the overall program’s objectives through varied contributions and approaches in each course. 

**An aligned curriculum: Components and their relationship**

  
In an aligned and cohesive curriculum, all components, including Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs), teaching strategies, learning materials, and assessment tasks, are aligned to ensure the achievement of CLOs, as depicted in Figure 1.

The alignment process involves:

- Clearly defining CLOs to set teaching goals and curriculum objectives for reaching the outcomes.
- Choosing teaching and learning activities that support, guide, and motivate learners towards achieving the CLOs.
- Engaging learners actively in these activities.
- Using assessment methods that both demonstrate learning outcomes and provide valuable feedback to enhance learning.
- Evaluating learner performance against the learning goals with clear and achievable criteria.

For example, in the initial year of a program, a course may emphasise knowledge acquisition with less focus on skills and competencies. However, as learners progress through the program, there is a heightened emphasis on applying acquired knowledge and developing associated skills and competencies. Consequently, individual courses serve distinct purposes, and it is the cumulative learning from all courses that empowers learners to achieve the overarching PLOs.


A learner's program comprises multiple courses designed to collectively provide learning opportunities that help acquire essential knowledge, skills, and competencies. These courses include assessments to demonstrate the achievement of Program Learning Outcomes (PLOs).

The program team's role is to curate a mix of courses that ensure comprehensive coverage and depth, adhering to AQF standards. Creating Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs) involves planning teaching, learning, and assessment activities together to offer a well-rounded learning experience, enabling learners to meet and demonstrate the required outcomes


Effective CLOs set clear achievement goals for learners and guide the development of relevant, authentic assessments. These assessments enable learners to demonstrate their acquired knowledge, skills, and attitudes by the course's end.

- **Design process:** Starts with PLOs that encapsulate the broad learning goals, considering inputs from stakeholders and compliance with standards.
- **Assessment alignment:** CLOs, which are assessed across the study program, are crucial for measuring the achievement of PLOs. They are introduced, reinforced, and expanded throughout the curriculum.
- **Teaching and learning:** Activities are designed to support learners in successfully completing assessments, ensuring all PLOs are met by program end.
- **Curriculum mapping:** Ensures balanced development of PLOs and regular review of PLOs and CLOs to stay updated with stakeholder needs and policy changes.


The table below sums up a range of strategies to assist you with writing your own CLOs. All strategies should be carefully considered.

**Define Learner Achievements** 

- Focus on what learners must know or achieve by the end of the course.  
- Ensure CLOs clearly convey the skills, abilities, and knowledge gained. 

**Starting Point** 

- Use an introductory statement like, “Upon completing this course, learners will be able to…” 

**Use of Verbs** 

- Employ a specific action verb from an educational taxonomy (e.g., Bloom's Revised Taxonomy, SOLO, Fink's - see Handout 17) that best describes the intended outcome.  
- Choose a verb that matches the course level and discipline, ensuring it is observable and measurable. 

**Simplicity, Clarity and Precision** 

- Aim for **_4-6 CLOs_** to balance thorough assessment without overwhelming learners. 
- Avoid ambiguous verbs like ‘know’, ‘understand’, or ‘appreciate’, which are difficult to measure objectively. 
- Use **_unambiguous language_** that clearly communicates expected knowledge and abilities by course end, and for easy comprehension by all stakeholders.
- Tailor the CLO to the **_right level_** of detail, depth, and breadth, considering complexity, theory evaluation, and material uncertainty. 

**Assessment Compatibility** 

- Ensure CLOs are **_specific and demonstrable_**_,_ rather than broad and program-level, to avoid assessment difficulties. 
- Ensure each CLO can be assessed within the course's assessment strategy. 

**Level Alignment** 

- Match CLOs with the course's Australian Qualifications Framework (AQF) level - [https://www.aqf.edu.au/framework/aqf-qualifications](https://www.aqf.edu.au/framework/aqf-qualifications)

we will imagine CLO as having three components:  

**_CLO_** _= **Performance** + **Condition/Context** + **Criterion**_

1. **Performance** - This component combines the **action verb** and **object of that verb (content focus)** to describe what a learner must be able to do in observable terms. _e.g., Classify organisms in the animal kingdom._   
     
2. **Conditions/context*** - This qualifies the performance further by giving a:  

a.             **condition** within which the performance will happen. e.g., _classify organisms in the animal kingdom from given charts and/or specimens._  

b.             and/or **context** on which the performance is applied. e.g., _classify organisms in the animal kingdom and relate them to their habitats._  

3. **Criterion** - Criterion describes the required level at which the learners are expected to perform. Criterion can be described in terms of accuracy (e.g., minimum/maximum), productivity level, time, and degree of excellence or level of detail.  _e.g., classify organisms in the animal kingdom into vertebrates and invertebrates._

*While _Condition_ and _Context_ are slightly different in focus, they sometimes overlap; hence, we've grouped them together.

The essential element for forming a learning outcome is the **Performance** component, combining an action verb with a content focus. **Condition/context** and **Criterion**, while not mandatory for defining a learning outcome, are optional elements that can significantly refine a CLO's specificity when included.

That said, the sequencing of these three components may vary depending on the CLO to make grammatical sense.

**Performance                                                                                Condition and/or context                                  Criteria**

**_Action verb_**               **_Content focus_**                                                                                 

**Identify**                     Organisms in the animal kingdom                                                       

**Identify**                     Organisms in the animal kingdom                                                      …according to their classes and orders

**Identify**           Organisms in the animal kingdom           …from picture charts  

**(Performance condition)**                                                              

**Identify**                     Organisms in the animal kingdom                   …and relate them to their habitats **(context of application)**               

**Identify**           Organisms in the animal kingdom           …from picture charts  

**(Performance condition)**                                                             …according to their classes and orders


Using all the components identified above, we could revise the previous CLO into:

_Classify organisms in the animal kingdom, from picture charts, according to their classes and orders, and relate them to their habitats._  

From this CLO, it is clear:  

- What the learners are expected to perform: _Classify organisms in the animal kingdom._  
- Under what condition they are to perform it: _Using charts._  
- At what level of detail they are to do it: A_ccording to classes and orders._  
- What the context of application of the performance is: _Habitats._  

This structure aids in assessment and highlights teaching needs, such as requiring picture charts. It demonstrates how component selection adjusts CLO specificity, vital in vocational education and professionally accredited programs, without needing all components for a clear performance standard.

**Another example**

Revisiting the previous Consumer Behaviour course example, we might have these two examples as part of its set of CLOs:  

 _“**By the end of the course, learners will be able to:**_  

1. _Evaluate the influence of perception, motivation, attitudes, and lifestyle factors on consumer behaviour._
2. _Make recommendations for marketing strategies based on these insights._  
3. _Interpret research findings on consumer behaviour._
4. _Apply the findings to real-life marketing scenarios.”_  

While the CLOs #1 and #3 do not have a **condition** nor a specific **criterion**, the context of application does provide us indirectly with an expected standard of performance, in which they scaffold the context for the CLO that follows (i.e., #2 and #4 respectively).

In short, you are looking to ensure the CLOs help learners know what the course requires, actually reflect what the course is about, they are well written and make sense, and there are no gaps or overlaps.

## Course-Level Rubric 
The Course Level Rubric outlines the performance level for each of the Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs). It provides explicit descriptors for each outcome at each notation from High Distinction through to Fail; the Pass level is particularly important as this is the level at which you are saying the student has demonstrated the CLO at an acceptable level and can continue with their studies. **If your course uses the Non-Graded-Pass format, please only use the Pass column in the Course Level Rubric.**

When developing a Course rubric, and academic is asked to follow these steps:

1. Review the CLO and Identify Performance Levels: Define and describe what the Pass level entails in terms of quality, depth of understanding, and skill application. Remain true to the verbs in the original CLOs.
2. Define Criteria for Each Level: Break down each learning outcome into measurable components and specify what is required at each level of achievement. At least three bullet points per CLO that are tangible and actionable.
3. Add Explicit Examples: Minimise subjectivity by including bracketed examples alongside key adjectives and verbs.
4. Use Precise and Technical Language: Ensure consistency and clarity in terminology suitable for the academic level of the course.
Then focus on the descriptors and subskills for the HD/D/C/P/F levels.
5. Review the CLO and Pass level descriptors to Identify Performance Levels: Define and describe what the HD/D/C/P/F levels entail in terms of quality, depth of understanding, and skill application. Remain true to the verbs in the original CLOs. Do not modify the Pass standards
6. To distinguish levels of performance between the HD/D/C/P/F levels, you should consider the relevance of accuracy, depth of explanation, breadth of research, application of concepts, range of context, coherence of arguments or explanations, relevance, use of examples, justification and reasoning, use of terminology, independence, methodology, awareness or nature of errors, or consistency. Remember to keep the Verbs in line with the CLO and Pass level.
7. Check that each level of descriptor uses descriptive statements rather than just modifying one work (e.g., changing basic to good); Minimise the use of vague terms (e.g. excellent, adequate, solid, proficient); Use qualifiers that clarify the expected standard (e.g. thoroughly, clearly, insightfully);Use positive language and avoid deficit language (e.g. lacks depth).
8. Ensure that the language in the fail column is positive, i.e., it says cannot yet, rather than fails to.

**Course Rubric Principles**

Course rubrics are a tool that articulates what we expect a student to do if we say they have passed the course’s outcomes. The course rubric criteria are comprised of Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs), and the pass level descriptors define ‘what we expect a student to do’ if they have attained the outcomes. Course rubrics can bridge the gap between CLOs and assessment rubrics, the idea being that assessment criteria work together cumulatively to evidence the course rubric criteria. In this way, the course rubric functions as a constructive alignment tool. This also has beneficial implications for course development, because if the pass criteria are made clear, by deconstructing the CLOs into smaller, specific subskills, these can serve as signposts in the teaching sequence.

Course rubrics are student-facing to offer insight into achievement levels for each CLO but are not intended for grading. This distinction would be useful for a course to state explicitly so as to educate learners to the idea of the course rubric’s purpose.

Course rubrics cannot capture everything there is to know about a course, but should inform the reader with enough detail to illustrate how the attainment of the outcomes impacts the broader program: if a learner can do A, then the next course can begin with B. The course content and assessments will provide further detailed guidance beyond what is outlined in the course rubrics.  

_The following principles are designed to guide you in developing your course rubric. Since each rubric has unique nuances, we encourage you to connect with your CED for tailored advice on applying these principles effectively._

**General Principles for Constructing Course Rubrics:**  
  

**1. Verbs:**

a.       The Pass column should always reflect the verb from the Course Learning Outcome (CLO), or should use a synonym that demonstrates similar meaning. At a minimum, students should be able to demonstrate the skill or knowledge as described in the CLO to achieve a passing grade.

b.    Verbs should be directly observable and placed at the start of the criteria for clarity.

**2. Level-specific descriptions:**

a.    Each range (HD, D, C, P, F) should include 2- 4 bullet points breaking down the CLO into its subset skills. The subset skills in the pass level represent steps in the progression of learning towards the fulfilment of the outcome. If an outcome is comprised of many skills, the bullet points should represent broader themes.

b.       The descriptions should focus on the tangible evidence required to meet the expectations for that particular grade, such as the depth of analysis, understanding, or application of knowledge. It should not just be a reframing or rephrasing of the outcome.  

c.     The higher ranges (D and HD) should not include skills beyond the course’s scope.

d.    Ensure that each bullet point includes one central idea and avoids conjunctions to maintain clarity.

**3. Progression across ranges:**

a.       The grading ranges (HD, D, C) should reflect a clear progression from the Pass level in either **complexity** (e.g., higher-order verbs such as “analyse” or “evaluate” versus “describe”) or **quantity** (e.g., increasing breadth or scope of the task). 

b.    Emphasise what the student _can_ do at each level. Note, that this will not be the case in the Fail column (see 4a)

c.     Avoid where possible using ambiguous terms like "better" or "more comprehensive" that could be interpreted subjectively. Instead, define progression using measurable and observable criteria.

**4. Fail criteria:**

a.    The _Fail_ column should ideally begin with “Not yet able to…” followed by a clear description of what the student has not yet demonstrated at the _Pass_ level.  

**5. Clarity:**

a.    Use clear, objective, and measurable language that emphasises demonstrable skills and outcomes. Use specific quantities or examples where appropriate. Avoid if possible general or subjective statements (e.g., "some," "many," "good," "better," "best").

b.    Avoid jargon or overly technical terms that could obscure meaning.

c.     Ensure the rubric language is student-friendly and easily understood without requiring additional interpretation.

3.3 Mandatory requirements  
Mandatory requirements are those that students must meet before they are able to undertake the  
assessment in a course. For example, it may be mandatory to have completed an induction into a  
workplace-based learning environment. Mandatory requirements will be approved as part of the

3.4 Hurdle requirements  
Some courses will have assignments that must be completed to a satisfactory standard to pass that  
course. These hurdle requirements can be applied to graded and non-graded assignments.  
Hurdle requirements must be approved by [role] as part of the course design and justified by clear  
rationale based on:  
 safe practice or professional competencies  
 registration or accreditation requirements  
 the need to certify learning for quality assurance and academic integrity purposes.  
Due to the significant consequences to students of failing a hurdle, including failure because of  
[academic misconduct], the choice to have a hurdle requirement should be carefully considered.  
Any non-graded assignments in graded courses will have 0% weight and will be a hurdle  
requirement.  
Graded assignments or assignment groups may only have a hurdle requirement if they are  
worth 20% or more of the course grade.  
No more than 60% of the total assessment for a graded course can be subject to hurdle  
requirements.  
The consequence of not meeting a hurdle requirement is one of the following:  
 in a graded course, when a student’s total for the course is 50% or greater, a course grade of  
Failed Hurdle (FH) and a total of 49%  
 in a graded course, when a student’s total for the course is less than 50%, a course grade of Fail  
(F) with the total unchanged  
 in a non-graded course, a course grade of Fail (F).

as described in the [Variations to Assessment Procedure], will have 5% of the total mark deducted  
for every day (or part thereof) the assignment is overdue, for a maximum of 7 calendar days. After  
7 calendar days the assignment will not be accepted for marking.  
Some assignment types, such as test or group assignments, are not eligible for late submission.

This is a working document to support the development of the Curriculum Management System, CurV, for  
Adelaide University. It is expected that elements of it will eventually form the basis of a document  
supporting the Adelaide University assessment policy and procedures.  
An assessment taxonomy is proposed for Adelaide University that will categorise assignments (see below) in  
CurV. Assessment data from CurV will populate course outlines and may be used to create assignments in  
the course Canvas site.  
Aligned with the assessment framework, a taxonomy will enable learning by clearly and consistently  
communicating assessment processes, improving student outcomes and learning experience. Exposing a  
range of assignment types will support the design of assignments that are fit for purpose, authentic,  
meaningful, and inclusive of diverse students. A taxonomy will support continual improvement and  
accountability by enabling reporting across courses and programs.  
Background: Exams  
Exams will be an important part of assessment at Adelaide University. They will take many forms from  
traditional written papers to professional simulations to practical exams held in laboratories and computing  
suites. The common features of these exams are that they are held under carefully controlled conditions to  
ensure consistency and security, and they are centrally approved and scheduled. Rather than define an  
assessment type for every different kind of exam (practical exam, music performance exam…) this taxonomy  
acknowledges that any type of assessment can be an exam if it satisfies the following definition:  
Exams occur at a scheduled time approved by [a role to be determined], typically at the end of the  
teaching period although exceptions are possible. During an exam, students are required to complete  
tasks independently, with access only to specified resources, and within a specified timeframe. All  
students in an exam are subject to the same conditions, except where variations have been approved  
under an access plan. Exams are invigilated to prevent academic misconduct and to maintain consistent  
conditions for all students.  
Background: Assignments and Assignment Groups  
In Canvas, the term assignment is used for all assessment tasks and assignments are grouped together in  
assignment groups. The terms assignment and assignment group are built into Canvas and have therefore  
been consistently adopted in the Adelaide University assessment procedures.  
Assignments are assessment tasks. Assignments can be graded, non-graded, or purely formative.

An Assignment Group is a group of related assignments that together contribute a percentage  
weighting of a student’s course grade.  
The term assessment will still be used, for example when referring to assessment in general, but assignment  
will be used instead of assessment task, assessment activity, or assessment for items submitted for marking  
and feedback.  
Each assignment has a submission due date, receives a grade1 (which can be expressed as a letter grade,  
points, percentage…), and appears as a column in the grade book. Educators should give assignments names  
that are meaningful to their students and consistent with discipline norms  
Assignments are grouped together in assignment groups. An assignment group can contain a single  
assignment. Weights are assigned to assignment groups to determine their contribution to the final grade  
for the course. It is possible for the assignments within a group to contribute differently to the grade for the  
assignment group2. As for assignments, educators should give assignment groups names that are  
meaningful to their students and consistent with discipline norms  
Partitioning assessment into assignments and assignment groups is a design activity that requires care and  
judgement. An important consideration is clear communication of the assessment requirements for  
students. Another consideration is to enable analytics and reporting from which useful conclusions can be  
drawn to support and assure learning and continuous improvement of teaching quality. The choice is also  
influenced by the capabilities of Canvas.  
An assignment group may be used for assignments that are repeated across a course. For example, a course  
may include 3 assessed practicals. Each practical could be an assignment within the assignment group called  
“Practicals”.  
Alternatively, an assignment group can be used to connect activities that are stages in a larger piece of work.  
For example, the assessment in a course may include a written case study which is submitted as a draft for  
feedback, a final version, and a short presentation to the class. The draft, final version, and presentation  
would each be assignments. Together they could be in an assignment group called “Case Study”.  
Another use of an assignment group is where an assessment contains parts that involve different kinds of  
activity. For example, a course may include a written examination consisting of 2 parts: Part A, a set of  
multiple-choice questions; and Part B, a set of short response questions. Each part could be one assignment  
and together they could be an assignment group called “Exam”.  
Assignment groups can also be used to facilitate student choice in assessment. Students might be given the  
option of responding to an inquiry question with a written, video, or interview response. The alternatives

The remainder of this document describes a taxonomy for assignments. Each assignment has fields

communication with students, is the top priority. The others are in no particular order.  
1. Communication with students: important information about what to expect from an assignment,  
including before a student enrols in a course, should be captured as assignment fields so that it  
appears in the course outline.  
2. Useful for reporting: aspects of assessment that are useful for quality assurance, continuous  
improvement, or reporting to stakeholders (e.g. for a program review or accreditation submission)  
should be captured as fields.  
3. Needed for administrative support: fields may be used to identify assignments that require  
administrative support such as central scheduling.  
4. Where governance is required: aspects of assignments that should be subject to approval as part of  
the course design process should be included as fields so they can be managed using CurV’s built-in  
approval workflows.  
5. Consistent with Canvas grade book: where possible CurV fields should align with the structure and  
terminology of the Canvas grade book so that there is consistent information in the grade book and  
course outline. This may also support automatic creation or checking of assignments in the Canvas  
grade book.  
Category  
The category field expresses, in broad terms, the primary activity undertaken by students in an assignment.  
A more detailed expression of this is captured in a type field described below. Having both category and type  
enables reporting at coarse and fine levels of detail. For example, one might investigate the proportion of  
written assignments in a program before examining the break-down of different types of written  
assignment.  
An assignment can be of one category only. Note that the intent of this limitation is to simplify analytics and  
reporting, not to stifle diversity or innovation in assessment.

Some assessments might not fit neatly into one category. In this case there are two possibilities: the  
assessment might be expressed as multiple assignments within an assignment group; or the category could  
be chosen that most closely describes most of the activity in the assignment.

Type  
The type expresses the activity students will undertake in an assignment. An assignment can be of one type

The list of proposed types has been developed in line with the following principles:  
1. Assignment types are agnostic to discipline. They are expressed in general terms, and each could  
conceivably be applied in any module, course, or program.  
2. The type clearly describes what students can expect from the assignment.  
3. Assignment types are independent of other fields in the taxonomy.  
4. The number of assignment types in the taxonomy should be small enough that they can be clearly,  
consistently, and unambiguously applied; it should be large enough that there is meaningful  
coverage of current and potential future assessment practice.  
An assignment can be of one category only. Once again, the intent is to simplify analytics and reporting. It is  
acknowledged that not every assignment will find a type that is a perfect fit and that on occasions a ‘best fit’  
that captures the predominant activity in an assignment will be most appropriate.

Ai proofing assessments:

Iterative submission How does this support integrity? Students submit drafts of their work either for formative feedback or to demonstrate their process of drafting the assignment at the time of final submission. This approach also supports students with their time management and reduces the chances that they will resort to cheating due to time pressures. How can I achieve this? • Individual elements of an assessment can be submitted in stages, for example an outline, an annotated bibliography and drafts in different modes such as video or audio. • A portfolio approach is one way to organise iterative tasks. • Effective and efficient feedback is vital for the success of this approach. The Feedback for Learning project identifies important four design factors. • Cadmus is an online assessment platform which guides students through the development of their assessment while providing teachers with analytics for monitoring student progress. • If you are concerned about the time effective formative feedback can take, the Feedback Fruits Peer Review tool enables student-to-student feedback on formative assessment tasks. • Where possible, require students to submit DOCX files instead of PDFs, as a DOCX file can yield useful information such as the dates and times it was created and last modified, as well as the total time spent writing / editing.


Oral assessment How does this support integrity? This approach can be used as a whole assessment or a short check of the student’s learning and approach after they have submitted an assessment. How can I achieve this? • Oral assessment can be delivered on-campus or online. • Pecha Kucha is an effective and possibly less time-consuming approach to student presentations. • Interactive oral assessment has been used at Griffith University to create final assessments that uphold academic integrity and provide assessment security at scale while also developing students’ professional skills. • Pre-recorded or asynchronous submissions alone may be less effective to deter misconduct, as the production of slides or scripts can be outsourced, and you cannot question the student on their presentation

Exam or in-class assessment focused on higher-order thinking How does this support integrity? Current gen AI tools have improved at responding to open-ended questions, so it is important to design questions that require students to demonstrate higher-order thinking. How can I achieve this? • Guidance on writing questions for in-person and online proctored exams is available on the Learning and Teaching website. • AI tools could be used to generate text or programming code which students would be asked to improve upon. • Offering students the opportunity to develop their own research question (in consultation with you) in the lead up to an invigilated session in which they report on the outcomes and respond to prompts can support engagement and integrity. Problem or projectbased assessments How does this support integrity? Assessments that emphasise process, personalisation, and iterative feedback make it harder for students to rely solely on gen AI outputs. How can I achieve this? • The Introduction to Learning and Teaching course covers the basics of problem-based learning (in the Approaches to active learning module). • This guide provides a brief outline of the benefits, challenges and design considerations for project-based assessments. • Be aware that any type of assignment, including a whole project with multiple components, can be outsourced. A project might work well in combination with an iterative submission or multimedia submission. F2F in-class quizzes How does this support integrity? Quizzes that are given in class with the instructor acting as invigilator would be more difficult for students to cheat on with AI. How can I achieve this? • Some considerations in taking this approach would be: the level of learning students can demonstrate in a quiz format and therefore what weight this task should carry; if the requirement to be in class for an assessment disadvantages any students and whether alternative arrangements or Reasonable Adjustments would need to be made; implications for administrating these assessments, for example: what are the risks of paper based quizzes and whether students can be asked to complete them on a device in class. • You could also talk to an LEI Education Technologist about technologies to secure quizzes or assessments, such as the Respondus LockDown Browser.

Assessments which require students to refer to unique resources, notes from class discussion and current events How does this support integrity? Since gen AI tools cannot as easily replicate personalised, contextdependent input (such as class conversations), students cannot rely solely on them for comprehensive answers. How can I achieve this? • Assessment tasks should ask students to demonstrate their learning in ways that gen AI tools cannot easily replicate. See the ‘Practices we recommend’ section of this guide to adapting writing tasks for more detail on these approaches. • You could ask students to connect new knowledge, such as current news events, with coursespecific concepts discussed in class. Use gen AI as part of the assessment task How does this support integrity? Embracing gen AI technology enables students to develop AI literacy and to demonstrate analytical and critical thinking skills which the AI cannot do. How can I achieve this? • Use the AI in Assessment Guidelines as a starting point in communicating with your students about your expectations for how they use gen AI tools in an assessment. • Give students the option to create a response to an assessment prompt with an AI tool which they will then analyse for flaws and limitations and improve upon. You might want to model this critique in your teaching. • The University Library’s Gen AI Masterclass is a great 6-part introduction to using gen AI tools effectively and responsibly • Ask students to export a transcript of their interaction with gen AI and submit this alongside their assignment. This will help you to understand how they developed, checked and edited the information they retrieved from gen AI. • Keep in mind that many gen AI tools may not always be free to use. This has implications for equitable access for students. You will also need to ensure that students are aware of the University’s Generative AI IT Security Guidelines.


Personalised, adaptive, negotiated and authentic assessment tasks How does this support integrity? Authentic assessments require students to engage deeply with course material and demonstrate their unique understanding and application of knowledge How can I achieve this? • Simple examples of authentic assessment could be to ask students to demonstrate a laboratory or other applied procedure, or to create and present a diagrammatic representation of concepts. • Student Negotiated or Choice of Assessment (ucd.ie) • Assessing with Role Play and Simulation | UNSW Teaching Staff Gateway • More assessment design guidance can be found on the Learning and Teaching website. Flipping learning and teaching How does this support integrity? If students can engage with content in their own time, the in-class time can be used for active working towards assessment. This can facilitate peer learning, and you can be available to answer questions and get insight into student progress on tasks. How can I achieve this? • University of Adelaide colleagues created a template for designing flipped learning as part of an Office for Learning and Teaching Development and Innovation Project Grant. • You can learn more about the flipped approach and see examples in the ADEPT Designing Blended Learning self-enrol, self-paced course.

## Assessment Rubric 
All assessments require a rubric or marking guide (except where it simply doesn't make sense e.g., a multiple choice quiz).


In some discilpines however rubrics are not the best choice for example in stem, e.g., _In Engineering and IT, assessment design often focuses on authentic, applied, and standards-based judgement rather than criterion-based textual descriptions of quality. While rubrics and marking guides are considered good practice in many text-based disciplines, they are not always the most appropriate or effective tool in Engineering/IT contexts._

_For these disciplines, performance is often assessed through technical accuracy, functionality, and adherence to specifications or standards, where outcomes can be objectively verified or demonstrated (e.g., code runs, design meets tolerances, system functions as intended). In such cases, detailed rubrics or even marking guides can risk over-prescribing or constraining professional judgement and may not reflect the authentic assessment practices of the profession._

_In some Engineering, IT, and mathematically based subjects, the marking framework can only be finalised after the assessment is written, once the specific solution pathways and answer tolerances are confirmed. This ensures that the marking is valid, accurate, and aligned with the final version of the task. Developing a rubric or marking guide too early in these contexts can lead to inconsistencies or the need for substantial revision once the actual task is complete._

_The university’s Assessment Policy and Procedures do not require the use of rubrics or marking guides, only that marking processes are transparent, consistent, and aligned with learning outcomes. In Eng/IT, this can be achieved through clearly defined criteria, annotated exemplars, structured feedback, or other discipline-appropriate methods that demonstrate fairness and reliability without necessarily using a traditional rubric._

On the topic of different types of rubric:
The rubric types available for you to create in myLearning are detailed in the tabs below, using examples for 'making breakfast in bed' adapted from Gonzalez (2014). Which rubric type you choose will depend on _what_ you are assessing and the reason _why_ you are assessing it.

What is an analytic rubric?

Analytic rubrics assess assignments using multiple distinct marking criteria. Each criteria is rated along a scale (e.g. HD, D, C, P, F), and each level on the scale has clearly-defined performance standards.
Analytic rubrics are most appropriate for complex assignments with different (distinct) parts that need to be assessed, or where you want to assess the demonstration of specific academic skills (e.g. development rubrics), such as for essays and reports.

What is a holistic rubric?
Holistic rubrics assess all aspects of an assignment together as a whole using a single marking criterion. This criterion won't usually include a description, and instead will include scaled rating levels that clearly define different levels of overall competency on the assignment.

Holistic rubrics are most appropriate for when you want to provide feedback to learners on their overall level of performance on an assignment, rather than their performance against multiple criteria. 

What is a pass/fail rubric?
Pass/fail rubrics assess whether learners have demonstrated competency in a skill/task or series of skills/tasks. They are set up with one marking criteria per competency, and two ratings per criteria which clearly define what is required to demonstrate competency (one for 'pass' and one for 'fail').

Pass/fail rubrics are most common for non-graded assignments, but can be used for any competency-based assignment where there is a clear 'pass' or 'fail' and no in-between. You may also use the same pass/fail structure for complete/incomplete-style checklists.


What is a marking guide?
Marking guides assess assignments using multiple distinct marking criteria. Performance standards are defined in marking criteria descriptions, as marking guides do not include rating scales (e.g. no HD, D, C, P, F scale). Point scores are provided against each marking criterion, though written feedback may also be provided.

Marking guides are useful when marking an assessment by its sections (e.g. introduction, results, discussions etc.), rather than by skills demonstrated across the submission (e.g. writing or communication skills), which is where an analytic rubric may be more appropriate.

(You may be familiar with using marking guides solely as marking aides for yourself, and not as means of providing feedback to learners. Marking guides created using myLearning rubrics would be used for providing feedback to learners.)

What is a single-point rubric?
Single-point rubrics assess assignments using multiple distinct marking criteria. Performance standards are defined in marking criteria descriptions, as single-point rubrics do not include rating scales (e.g. no HD, D, C, P, F scale). Only written feedback is provided.

Normally, single-point rubrics present performance standards in the centre of the rubric, then leave space to provide feedback on where learners need to improve/where learners have exceeded expectations either side of this criterion.
